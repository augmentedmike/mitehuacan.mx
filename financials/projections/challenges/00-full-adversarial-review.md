# Adversarial Review — From the Ground in Puebla

*System prompt for this review: I am a business operator who has built and sold
products in Puebla, Mexico for 15+ years. I have watched five gringo-style
startup projections die on the rocks of Mexican reality. I speak in specifics,
not optimism. I respect the hustle but I do not respect wishful math.*

---

## The 10 Differences That Invalidate US-City Projections

These are not surface differences. They are structural. Every assumption below
flows from one or more of these.

### 1. The cash economy is not a detail — it is the operating system

In a major US city, a business pays $500/mo by credit card and it's frictionless.
In Tehuacán, at least 40% of small businesses operate primarily in cash.
They don't have a "business bank account." They don't have a credit card.
SPEI exists but requires CLABE, banking app, and internet. Getting paid $300/mo
from 200 small businesses is a **collections operation**, not a billing one.
The projections assume zero friction on payment. That is wrong.

### 2. "Subscription" is a foreign concept in this market

US businesses understand monthly SaaS subscriptions. In Tehuacán, advertising
is bought one-shot: perifoneo today, radio spot this week, flyer this month.
The mental model of "I pay $300 every month forever" does not exist for most
micro-businesses. You are not selling a subscription. You are selling a new
habit. Conversion is slower. Churn is higher. Re-activation is harder.

### 3. The 10-minute close is a fantasy

The business/marketing-plan.md script ("Walk in. Phone out. Close in 10 minutes.") works in
San Francisco. In Tehuacán, the owner may not be there. If they are, you will
spend 10 minutes on greeting, asking about their family, and accepting a Coke
before you can even show your phone. First visit close rate is 3-5%, not 25-35%.
You need 3-5 visits to close most businesses. This alone doubles the cost of
sale and halves the monthly output.

### 4. Facebook is not a competitor — it is the internet

For most Tehuacán residents and business owners, Facebook *is* the internet.
They do not browse websites. They do not search Google for businesses. They
search Facebook. If mitehuacan.mx is not on Facebook — meaning a real presence
with content, not just a page — it does not exist for a large portion of your
audience. The plan assumes a standalone web app will drive adoption. History
in Mexico shows that standalone apps without a Facebook distribution strategy
fail to reach critical mass in small cities.

### 5. The informal economy is not a niche — it is the majority

INEGI estimates 55-60% of employment in Puebla is informal. This means the
business does not issue facturas, does not pay taxes formally, and critically,
**does not want to appear on a digital platform that might attract SAT attention**.
The 28,727 DENUE businesses are the *formal* count. The real number of
commerce points in Tehuacán may be 2x higher. But the informal ones will be
harder to sell and harder to retain because digital visibility carries risk
for them, not reward.

### 6. Family decision-making is not an edge case

Many businesses in Tehuacán are family-run. The person behind the counter may
be the son/daughter, the employee, or the aunt. They cannot decide. The owner
comes by in the evening. Or the decision goes to a family meeting on Sunday.
You cannot close on the first visit. You cannot close on the second visit if
the owner wasn't there. The 250-interactions-per-month model counts visits,
not decisions.

### 7. Seasonal cash flow is severe and predictable

Christmas (December) is strong. January is the leanest month — businesses are
recovering from holiday spending and paying off debts. September (vuelta a
clases) is another lean period. Agricultural communities around Tehuacán have
cycles tied to harvests. A business that signs in December may struggle to
pay in January. The projections show a smooth upward curve. Real revenue will
be lumpy with predictable dips.

### 8. Advertising alternatives are cheap and understood

Perifoneo (a car with speakers) costs $500-1,000 per day and reaches an entire
neighborhood. Radio spots on local stations cost $300-800 per spot and reach
thousands. Flyers handed out at the intersection cost $1,000 for 5,000 copies.
These are known quantities with known ROI. A digital sponsorship on a new
platform with unknown traffic is a harder sell than a projection spreadsheet
acknowledges. The price needs to beat the perceived value of known alternatives,
not the cost of Facebook ads.

### 9. WhatsApp is the platform. Your platform is a tool.

Businesses in Mexico do not open websites. They open WhatsApp. The primary
digital storefront for a small business in Tehuacán is: (1) Facebook page,
(2) WhatsApp number. If mitehuacan.mx does not deeply integrate with WhatsApp —
not just a button, but actual workflow — the business profile will feel like
a secondary tool they manage for your benefit, not theirs. Adoption will
reflect that.

### 10. Heat, distance, and logistics are real costs

Tehuacán is hot (30-35°C in summer). Commercial corridors are spread out.
A salesperson walking 6 hours a day covering 8-10 visits is not realistic
for 5 days a week, 52 weeks a year. Two afternoons per week is the limit
before burnout. This caps the addressable visit volume well below 250/month.
The Year One sales projection assumes peak productivity from month one.
In reality, months 1-3 are learning the territory and building relationships,
months 4-6 reach partial productivity, months 7+ reach full productivity.

## Phase-by-Phase Challenges

### Phase A — Route Sponsorships

#### Assertion: 3 new sponsors in month 1, ramping to 13 by month 6

**Challenge:** Month 1 includes zero time for relationship building. The
salesperson needs to learn the routes, identify which combis run them, find
the owners, build rapport, and then sell. Realistically, month 1 produces
0-1 sponsors (if the salesperson starts with the owner's own network).
Month 2 produces 1-2. Month 3 produces 2-3. The ramp is 3 months delayed.

**Adjusted estimate:** Month 1: 0-1. Month 2: 1-2. Month 3: 2-3. Month 4: 4. 
Not 3-5-7 for months 1-3.

#### Assertion: 25-35% close rate

**Challenge:** This is the single most dangerous assumption in the entire
projection. In a market where trust must be earned over multiple visits,
where the decision maker is often absent, and where the product is a new
category (digital sponsorship), a 25-35% close rate is achievable only if
you count *introductions from trusted sources*, not cold visits. The cold
visit close rate in Tehuacán for a new digital product is closer to 5-10%.

**Adjusted estimate:** 5-10% cold close. 20-25% on warm leads (referral,
second visit, introduced).

#### Assertion: $500/mo per location, 4 locations per sponsor = $2,000/mo average

**Challenge:** The business/marketing-plan.md pricing says founding rate is $250-400/mo per
location. The projection uses $500/mo — the *standard* rate — from month 1.
If the founding rate ($250-400) is actually charged for the first 100 sponsors,
the average is $325/mo, not $500/mo. With 4 locations, that's $1,300/mo per
sponsor, not $2,000. This alone cuts Phase A revenue by 35%.

**Adjusted estimate:** $325/mo average first year → $1,300/sponsor → 35% less
revenue than projected.

#### Assertion: 80% renewal rate Year 2

**Challenge:** Subscription churn in Mexico for new digital products aimed at
micro-businesses runs 40-60% in year one across comparable products I have
seen (directory listings, local advertising platforms). The 80% renewal
assumption would be optimistic for a US SaaS product. For a Mexican micro-
business product, it is unrealistic.

**Adjusted estimate:** 50-60% renewal. Year 2 active sponsors peak at 100-110,
not 181.

#### Net effect on Phase A:

| Metric | Projected | Adjusted |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 gross | $1,312,000 | $750,000-850,000 |
| Year 2 gross | $3,740,000 | $1,800,000-2,200,000 |
| Active sponsors (mo 24) | 181 | 100-120 |

### Phase B — Business Directory & Premium Profiles

#### Assertion: $300/mo per profile, zero commission, self-serve

**Challenge:** $300/mo for a business profile when the business already has
a free Facebook page with photos, hours, and WhatsApp is a tough sell. The
differentiator ("transit integration") is meaningful only to businesses on
a combi route, which is a subset of the 28K DENUE businesses. For businesses
not on a route, the profile is just a listing on a platform nobody has heard
of yet. The value prop is thin.

**Challenge on self-serve:** The projection assumes "no dedicated salesperson"
and "self-serve tier." In Tehuacán, self-serve adoption for paid digital
products is near zero among micro-businesses. You will need to hand-hold
every single signup. If there is no dedicated salesperson driving this, it
will not grow.

**Challenge on cross-sell assumptions:** 60% cross-sell to route sponsors
assumes the salesperson pitches both products in one visit. In the 10-minute
close model, there is no time for a second product. And if close rate drops
from 25% to 5% (my adjusted estimate), the base of sponsors to cross-sell
to is much smaller.

**Adjusted estimate:** Penetration reaches 80-100 profiles by month 24 (not
238). Year 2 revenue: $250,000-300,000 (not $651,000). The product works only
if a dedicated salesperson or channel pushes it.

---

### Phase C — Smart Search & Category Sponsors

#### Assertion: 600 addressable slots (15 categories × 20 routes × 2 tiers)

**Challenge:** Category exclusivity ("La Farmacia Recomendada de la Ruta 15")
presupposes that riders search by business category rather than by route
number or destination. This feature does not exist yet. When it ships, it
requires residents to change their behavior from "I need route 15" to "I
need a pharmacy on my way home." That is a significant behavior shift.

**Challenge on willingness to pay $1,500/mo for exclusivity:** A pharmacy in
Tehuacán might make $3,000-5,000/day in revenue. Asking for $1,500/mo —
potentially 1-2% of monthly revenue — for a "recommended" badge on a new
platform is a very hard sell. The US analogy (Yelp advertising, Google
Local Services) does not translate because the traffic is unproven.

**Challenge on inventory:** Not all 15 categories exist on all 20 routes.
A route serving a residential area may have 2 pharmacies, 0 dentists, and
1 hardware store. The "300 slots" number is theoretical max, not addressable.

**Adjusted estimate:** 60-80 slots by month 24 (not 165). Average price
closer to $400 (not $600) because most buyers choose featured over
exclusivity. Year 2 revenue: $300,000-400,000 (not $807,000).

### Phase D — Fiestas & Events Marketplace

#### Assertion: 75,000 households, 1% host a fiesta per year = 750 events

**Challenge:** The number of households hosting a *planned fiesta* (birthday,
quinceañera, boda, etc.) is probably higher than 1%. But the question is
whether those hosts will use a new digital platform to organize it. Currently,
fiesta planning in Tehuacán happens via WhatsApp groups, word of mouth, and
known vendors. The platform needs to offer something dramatically better than
a WhatsApp group. The US analogy (Evite, Paperless Post) does not apply
because WhatsApp already does this for free and everyone already has it.

**Challenge on vendor willingness to pay $500/mo:** A DJ in Tehuacán might
charge $2,000-4,000 per event. Paying $500/mo ($6,000/yr) for leads means
they need 2-3 additional events per year just to break even. If the platform
delivers 1-2 leads per month in early stages, the math works. If it delivers
1 lead per quarter, it does not. Vendor retention depends entirely on lead
quality, which depends on host adoption, which is uncertain.

**No mention of physical fiesta logistics:** Many fiestas in Tehuacán happen
in the home, at a salon de eventos, or in the street (with permits). The
platform needs to handle location logistics, which vary enormously.

**Adjusted estimate:** 40-50 vendors by month 24 (not 92). Year 2 revenue:
$200,000-250,000 (not $455,800).

---

### Phase E — Home Services Marketplace

#### Assertion: 2,000+ registered service professionals; 95 providers by month 24

**Challenge:** The home services marketplace model (Thumbtack, TaskRabbit)
faces a specific problem in Mexico: trust is personal. When a plumber is
needed, the homeowner asks their network — vecinos, familia, el compadre.
They do not search a platform. The platform needs to overcome the trust
barrier for both sides: homeowners need to trust that providers are vetted,
and providers need to trust that leads are real and worth paying for.

**Challenge on $400/mo membership:** A plumber in Tehuacán might make
$8,000-15,000/mo in revenue. Paying $400/mo (3-5% of revenue) for leads
that are unproven is risky from their perspective. Pay-per-lead at $100
feels safer but requires the platform to deliver enough volume to matter.

**Challenge on timing:** Month 13 assumes the platform has 2,000+ DAU by
then. If Phase A underperforms (see my adjusted estimates), user base grows
slower, which means fewer service requests, which means less value for
providers, which means lower retention.

**Adjusted estimate:** 50-60 providers by month 24 (not 95). Year 2 revenue:
$120,000-150,000 (not $232,000).

---

### Phase F — Tianguis & Local Commerce

#### Assertion: 10+ established tianguis. 100 vendors by month 24.

**Challenge:** Tianguis vendors are among the most informal businesses in
Mexico. Many do not have RFC, bank accounts, or smartphones. Paying $200-300/mo
for a digital profile when they pay $50-100/day for their physical stall is
a hard math problem. The value prop ("people can find you on the map") means
little when the vendor's customer base is walk-by traffic at the tianguis,
not digital discovery.

**Challenge on adoption:** Tianguis vendors are not browsing the internet
for business tools. Reaching them requires physical presence at the tianguis,
which means a different sales approach — and likely a different salesperson
who speaks the language of mercado vendors, not the language of tech.

**Adjusted estimate:** 30-50 vendors by month 24 (not 100). Average price
closer to $150/mo (not $250). Year 2 revenue: $40,000-60,000 (not $101,000).

---

### Phase G — Employment & Community

#### Assertion: 48 accounts by month 24 at ~$450/mo average

**Challenge:** This phase is the furthest out (month 19) and the most
speculative. The job board market in Tehuacán is dominated by Facebook
groups (there are multiple "empleos en Tehuacán" Facebook groups with
50,000+ members, all free), word of mouth, and the occasional OCC or indeed
listing for professional roles. A paid job board for Tehuacán-specific roles
needs to offer something these free channels do not. For most employers in
Tehuacán, Facebook groups work fine and cost nothing.

**Challenge on community events:** Events are promoted via Facebook events
and WhatsApp forwards. These are free, ubiquitous, and well-understood.
Paying $200/event for promotion on a new platform is a tough ask.

**Adjusted estimate:** 20-30 accounts by month 24. Average $300/mo (per-post
cheaper than subscriptions). Year 2 revenue: $25,000-35,000 (not $57,000).

## But Here Is What the Projection Gets Right

The adversarial review is not a prediction. It is a stress test. Let me be
fair about what is actually strong in these projections:

1. **The unit economics work if volume materializes.** $500/mo from a business
   that gets 10 additional customers per month is genuinely good ROI.
   The fundamental value prop (discovery during transit) is real.

2. **The sales process is well-designed for digital close.** WhatsApp contract
   + click-to-sign + instant pin publication is genuinely innovative for this
   market. It solves the "when can I see results?" objection elegantly.

3. **Phase 0 (QR stickers in combis) is the right starting point.** Organic
   discovery via QR codes on combis creates a usage loop that requires zero
   marketing spend. This is a real advantage over competitors who need to
   buy ads.

4. **The DENUE data is a real moat.** Having 28,727 pre-located businesses
   is a legitimate operational advantage. The prospect lists exist and are
   actionable. Most local advertising startups in Mexico skip this step.

5. **Barter sponsorships (Phase 0) are culturally smart.** Trading visibility
   for wall space is a concept Mexican micro-businesses understand and will
   accept. It seeds the map at zero cash cost.

## What the Projection Needs to Fix

1. **Double the sales cycle, halve the close rate.** Adjust months 1-6 for
   realistic ramp. Recalculate Year 1 with 5-10% cold close rate.

2. **Add a collections cost assumption.** If 40% of payments arrive late or
   require follow-up, that costs time and money. Budget for it.

3. **Foundation pricing must match business/marketing-plan.md.** Use $325/mo average, not
   $500/mo, for the first 100 sponsors. Recalculate.

4. **Add a Facebook distribution line item.** Either a Facebook integration
   budget or a content marketing cost. Without it, adoption is capped.

5. **Model seasonality.** Add January and September dips. Real revenue is
   lumpy. Investors prefer a projection that acknowledges this.

6. **Lower the churn assumption.** Use 50-60% for Year 1 renewals. The plan
   can beat this, but it should plan for it.

7. **Chunk the sales territory.** One salesperson cannot cover all of
   Tehuacán's 80 routes effectively in 6 hours/day. Propose a route-by-route
   rollout plan starting with the 10 highest-density corridors.

8. **Add cash collection infrastructure.** Plan for a weekly cobranza route.
   If you accept cash payments, someone needs to physically collect them.

---

*A projection that survives this review is worth building toward. One that
ignores it is a wish. The product is real. The need is real. The path needs
harder math.*
